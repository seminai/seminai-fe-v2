At the trade fair stand the salesperson says it three times: the new software "has artificial intelligence". The farm owner nods. The adviser with them wonders what that means in practice, for the spray record, for doses, for the week's work. **AI in farming** has become a label stuck on almost everything, and for that very reason it explains little.

Let us take the mystery out of it. What it is, what it can do today, how widespread it is and, above all, what it does not do.

## What it is, without jargon

The EU regulation on artificial intelligence, 2024/1689, gives a long definition. Boiled down: it is an automated system that, starting from the data it receives, **works out by itself how to produce a result**. A prediction, a text, a suggestion, sometimes a decision.

The key word is "works out". A traditional program follows rules written by a person: if the dose exceeds the limit, then warn. An AI system has been trained on many examples and **recognises patterns**: this spot on the leaf looks like those of a disease, this invoice line looks like a plant protection product.

Two limits follow, worth keeping in mind. The system is only as good as the examples it learned from. And it does not know your farm: it does not know that "the bottom field" is the vineyard along the ditch, unless someone tells it.

## What it can do today

Eurostat, the EU statistical office, splits AI technologies into eight families. For a farm they can be grouped into four actions:

- **reading and writing text**: extracting data from an invoice, summarising a document, answering a question;
- **recognising images**: a symptom on a leaf, a weed in a photo;
- **predicting**: estimating a yield or a risk from historical data, weather, satellite images;
- **driving machines**: robots and vehicles that move on their own in the field.

There is no shortage of farming examples. In February 2025 a seminar of the EU CAP Network on robotics and artificial intelligence gathered experience with guided irrigation and fertilisation, automated pest control, yield forecasting and satellite data analysis. Among the needs, the same seminar named a very concrete one: **training and skills**.

## How widespread it is

In 2025, 20% of EU enterprises with at least 10 employees used at least one AI technology, against 13.5% in 2024. The most common use is analysing written text, at 11.8%. Two details matter: the survey **does not cover agriculture**, and in 2025 it counts one more technology (generating images and sound), so the comparison with 2024 is indicative.
</ResearchNote>

So the figures describe industry and services, not farms. They work as a thermometer: they show how quickly these tools are entering daily work, including among suppliers, contractors and cooperatives that farms deal with.

In Italy, according to ISTAT, in 2025 16.4% of enterprises with at least 10 employees used at least one AI technology, double the 8.2% of 2024. Among those that do not use it, the first obstacle cited is **lack of skills**, named by 58.6%.

## What it does not do

It does not decide for you. A system that reads an invoice proposes a line, it does not sign it. A model that estimates a disease risk suggests a check, it does not choose the product. **Responsibility for the record stays with whoever keeps it**: Italian Legislative Decree 150 of 2012 requires the holder to sign the treatment register even when someone else filled it in.

It does not guarantee it is right. It makes mistakes differently from a person, sometimes with great confidence. That is why it matters to see where each proposal comes from and to correct it quickly.

It does not replace those who know the field. FAO, the United Nations food and agriculture agency, says so plainly too. At the AI summit in February 2026 its Director of Innovation, Vincent Martin, said AI in agriculture "must be rigorously tested, responsibly governed and sustainably financed". In July 2026 a FAO release added that having access to the tools does not mean getting results, and that without care small farms risk being left behind.

<InPratica>
- Ask the supplier which of the four things the tool does: read text, recognise images, predict or drive machines.
- Check that every proposal can be seen, corrected and confirmed before it becomes official data.
- Ask where the farm's data end up, who can use them and how they are exported.
- Allow time for learning: it is the first obstacle named by Italian businesses.
</InPratica>
