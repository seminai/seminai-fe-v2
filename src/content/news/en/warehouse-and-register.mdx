At the end of the season an adviser opens three piles. The first is invoices: what was bought, and when. The second is the notebook: what appears to have been used. The third is a stock sheet, updated when there was time. The three totals do not agree. A drum is missing. A pack is left that nobody remembers opening. A spray date comes before the invoice.

The following days are not agronomy. They are **making the totals close**. Quantities move, dates are corrected, someone phones to ask whether “that spray on the lower block was the new product or the old one”. In the end the numbers close. Confidence in what happened is a different matter.

## Where the thread breaks

The warehouse and the register are two views of the same gesture. Buy, keep, use, remain. If each of these words lives in a different file, **the thread breaks in predictable places**.

**The name does not match**. The invoice has the full trade name, the notebook has a short form, the shelf has a shortened label. They look like three products. They are one.

**The quantity does not match**. You buy in packs, you use litres or kilograms of product, you think in dose per hectare. Without one translation, the drum that is “finished” in the field story is still “half” on the sheet.

**The time does not match**. The spray is written on the day someone types, not on the day they went out. The purchase is recorded when the invoice arrives, not when the goods are on the shelf. An inspection reads the order of facts and asks whether it holds.

Farms that collect papers in a loose way struggle most at the turn of the season: what remains in December becomes March’s stock, but **only if someone wrote it down**. Otherwise you buy again, or you use a leftover the register does not know.

## Traceability, inside the gate as well

A review of food traceability recalls a simple definition: being able to **follow a product through the steps** it crosses. That following is possible if each step leaves a mark linked to the one before. Inside the farm the steps are the purchase, the stock and the use in the field. If one of these is missing, or is written on a list the others cannot see, the trail stops before it ever reaches a customer.
</ResearchNote>

The review talks about whole chains, from production to distribution. The piece in the farm’s hands is the same mechanism, smaller: one step, one mark, one link.

## One movement, not three transcriptions

When the invoice enters the archive, stock rises with a date and a product. When a spray is confirmed, stock falls by the quantity used, on the field named. The register **is not a second warehouse to type**. It is the story of what went out, read in order.

The year-end check then changes nature. You do not hunt for missing numbers. **You look at exceptions**: a line with no invoice, a product sitting too long, a quantity that does not fit the area sprayed. There are few of them, and they can be explained.

## How to read a gap without rewriting the season

A gap has few causes, and **it helps to look at them in order**. First the name: the same product written two ways. Then the unit: packs against litres. Then the date: goods arrived one day, invoice the next, spray written on the day of typing. Then the area: dose calculated on the whole plot, product used on only part of it. Only at the end does it make sense to talk about a forgotten use or a physical loss.

If you start from the last cause, you “fix” the notebook and hide the first four, which are writing errors, not field errors. An archive that keeps invoice, stock and spray on the same line shows **the cause while it is still small**. In March you correct a name. In December you correct a season.

A stock count does not have to be a textbook inventory. It can be a morning spent only on products the register calls finished and products the sheet calls plentiful. Those are the two most useful lies. If the “finished” product is on the shelf, a use was not written. If the “plentiful” product is not there, a purchase was counted twice or a use never entered the register. Starting from these two lists keeps the count short and repeatable at each change of season.

It is also worth deciding what the official stock is. **The physical shelf beats the sheet**, but only if someone looks at it on a dated day. A count made on the first of March, written in the archive, becomes the start of the next season. Without that date, the leftover is an opinion. With that date, the leftover is a fact from which purchases start, and purchases do not duplicate what was already there.

Seminai links purchase documents to the warehouse and, from there, to operations a person confirms. It does not promise that every shelf in the world is already counted. It promises **not to retype by hand** what the invoice and the spray have already said.
