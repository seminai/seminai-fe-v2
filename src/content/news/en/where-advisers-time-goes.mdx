An office that fills notebooks for many farms knows the proportion. A small, orderly farm takes a few hours. A farm that delivers envelopes when the season is over takes days. In between there are phone calls to complete what is missing, dose corrections, and the same invoice opened three times because three programs want it in three ways.

That time is **agronomic skill spent on transcription**. The person who can read a vineyard spends the afternoon matching a trade name to a spreadsheet row.

## Copying is the real load

The repeated gesture is not difficult. It is this: read a product, write it somewhere else, check you did not miss a digit, move to the next farm. On ten farms it is a morning. On a hundred it is the job.

Offices see it clearly. Removing the retyping is not a software whim. It is **the realistic way to follow more farms** without hiring only to copy, and without lowering attention exactly on the cases that should be looked at: organic, copper, the buffer, the product that is not in the store.

There is also the hidden time of corrections. You call the farm because the date does not fit. You call again because the photo is unreadable. You redo a fertiliser plan that was set on one sheet and then copied by hand into another. **A whole day can disappear** on a single farm, not because the plan is complex, but because the starting numbers were not in the same place.

Junior advisers, set to type without an ordered base, depend on someone checking every line. It is not a problem of people. It is a problem of material: if the line is born from nothing, all of it must be watched. If it is born from an invoice already read and a field already known, you watch the difference.

## A tool that adds a step does not save time

<ResearchNote
  label="What the research says"
  source="Rose and colleagues, 2016. Decision support tools for agriculture. Agricultural Systems."
  href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agsy.2016.09.009"
>
In the same study of farmers and advisers, whether a tool is adopted also depends on how much effort it takes and whether it sits inside work that is already expected, including the paperwork to hand in. A tool that forces you to retype what was already written elsewhere fails that test. **It costs time twice**: once when you use the usual program, and again when you pour the data across.
</ResearchNote>

For an office the sum is blunt. Every minute of pouring, multiplied by the farms followed, is **a minute taken from visits**, from useful calls and from the cases that are not routine.

## Look at the exceptions, do not redo the lines

If invoices, fields and notes flow into one archive, the adviser does not start from a blank sheet. They start from a suggestion: these products came in, these lines look like sprays, these quantities do not fit the area. Their time goes **to the exceptions and to confirmation**.

That is how an office can follow more farms with the same attention, not with less. The signature is not delegated to the program. The copy is.

## What remains an adviser’s work

It is worth saying this plainly, because the opposite fear is realistic: that removing the copies also removes the eye. It does not, if the eye moves to the questions the copy never asked. Is this product, on this field, at this point in the season, the right choice. Does the suggested dose respect the label and the production rules. Does the stock cover the round, or must you order. Do the copper years leave room. Is the neighbouring row organic, and is the buffer there.

These questions take minutes when **the facts are already on the table**, and hours when the facts must first be rebuilt. An office that measures its time only in “notebooks closed” does not see the difference. An office that measures how many farms it can really look at, during the season and not in January, sees it at once.

The relationship with people at the start of the job changes too. Instead of dictating lines, you review the exceptions together. The junior learns the strange cases, not the typing. The person who signs keeps signing. The program does not enter the field visit and does not replace the call in which you decide not to spray. It enters the stretch where, today, skilled people retype names.

You can measure the difference without a complicated dashboard. For a month, at the end of the day, note whether the time went **to copies or to judgments**. Copies: retyping, hunting a file, calling back for a fact that was already there. Judgments: choosing, correcting a suggestion, explaining a case. If copies still fill most of the sheet, the archive is not yet the place where facts meet. If judgments grow, the office is using people for the work they were asked to do.

Seminai is meant for this role: prepare the lines from documents and leave the judgment to the adviser. It is not a reason to reduce field skill. It is a reason not to waste it on transcription.
