It is Monday morning. An adviser needs to know whether a spray was done, with which product, and whether that product was actually in the store. The invoice is in an email. The notebook is in the office, or still in the van. The stock figure is on a spreadsheet. A voice message from the night before says “did the usual on the lower vineyard”, with no precise date and no product name.

None of these pieces is wrong on its own. Together they do not make a story. To answer a simple question you **open four places** and hope they agree. Often they do not.

## One season, many files

On many farms the facts of the same year live apart:

- invoices and delivery notes arrive on paper, as PDFs or as photos;
- field work is written on a scrap sheet, or sent as a message;
- the warehouse is updated later, when someone has time;
- the field notebook is closed near the end, when an inspection is coming;
- in the cellar, orders, delivery notes and payments take yet another path.

People who know the farm keep it in their head. The problem shows up when the season is long, when many farms are followed, or when an inspection asks for one day six months earlier. Memory is not enough, and the files do not talk.

This is not a failure of care. It is **how the work is split**. The person spraying is not sitting at the computer. The person buying is not the person filling the register. The person counting stock did not hear the evening message. Each one writes in the place at hand. A single story does not appear by itself.

## What breaks when the pieces stay apart

The first effect is time. Copying an invoice, then a spray, then a stock line means **writing the same product three times**. In an office that follows many farms, those hours multiply. The adviser spends the day fixing papers instead of looking at plants.

The second effect is the quiet mistake. A dose written on the whole plot, including roads and strips that are not sprayed, does not match what left the store. A spray dated before the product was bought is hard to explain. In organic farming, a product that is not allowed, written on the wrong plot, can **force that land back into conversion**.

The third effect is a decision taken late. If the notebook closes at the end of the year, nobody sees **the whole picture** during the season: what has already been used, what is left, what is no longer allowed. You repair it afterwards, when repair costs more.

Farm data does not arrive in one format. A review from Wageningen University separates what comes from business records, what comes from machines, and what comes from people, such as notes and messages. The hard part is not collecting more material. It is **making these sources readable together**, with a minimum of order and quality. Without that step, a pile of files does not help anyone decide.
</ResearchNote>

The review looks at very different farms, from sensors to accounts. The useful lesson, even for a farm with no connected tractor, is the same: the value is **in the link, not in the pile**.

## One place, not one more program

Keeping data in one place does not mean throwing away the software, the spreadsheet or the portal you already use. It means **a fact enters once and stays recognisable**.

When an invoice arrives, the products enter the warehouse with a name, a quantity and a date. When someone records a spray, that act reduces the stock and sits on the right field, on the area actually sprayed. When the register is closed, the lines are already there: they are read and confirmed, not reinvented from memory. In the cellar, the same order should not be rewritten for the delivery note and then again for the payment.

Artificial intelligence helps with the boring passage: it reads a crooked document, suggests a link, flags a dose or a date that does not fit. It **does not sign for anyone**. The person who knows the field looks at the suggestion and says whether it is the right one. Seminai is built around this idea: documents, fields and stock in the same archive, with a person confirming before a line becomes official.

## Where to start

You do not need to move ten years of archives in an afternoon. Start with **what already causes confusion every week**: product invoices, the notebook that is filled late, the stock that does not match. The rest of this series looks at those knots one by one, in the language of a farm, not of a software manual.
