An adviser has a vineyard map. The sheet says 4.2 hectares. Inside it there is a headland, an edge along the ditch, and two rows that will not be sprayed this year because the vines were pulled. If you buy and record the product on 4.2, the tank leaves too full, or the notebook declares **a dose that never happened in the field**.

It is a quiet mistake. You do not see it from the cab. You see it when label, area and stock are compared. It shows up often, because the “real” area is not written anywhere in a stable way: each person recalculates it, by hand, when it is needed.

## The whole map is not the treated field

The label of a plant protection product speaks of a quantity related to the area where the product is applied. National rules on sustainable use also ask for strips where you do not spray, especially near water. Those strips stay on the map sheet. **They do not stay in the dose calculation.**

The same happens with localised sprays and with passes made on only some rows. The dose **refers to what was wetted**. Marking the whole plot “for simplicity” shifts every account: the product leaving the shelf, the number of sprays, the label maximum, and, under stricter rules, the fertiliser plan as well.

People who follow many farms **lose hours on this subtraction**. Plot by plot, roads and unsprayable areas come off, the cadastral data is checked, the drawing is tidied. It is measuring work, done with office tools, repeated because the right number does not stay attached to the field.

## When the tool ignores the real gesture

A review of precision-agriculture tools notes that many stay unused because they are built far from the way farmers and advisers actually decide. A hectare total that knows nothing of ditches, excluded rows and buffer strips is that kind of tool: it asks for a clean number, while a spray is a gesture on part of the map. The paper does not calculate your buffers for you. It explains why a number detached from field work is not used, or is corrected by hand afterwards.
</ResearchNote>

The area to spray is not a surveyor’s detail. It is **the bridge between the label and the warehouse**. If the bridge is crooked, the “right” dose on the sheet is the wrong dose in the tank.

## A number written once

The first measure of the buffer, the ditch, the removed row, is work to do calmly, on the farm, with the person who knows the edge. It should not be redone at every spray. It should be attached to the field, with the date it was looked at, and **reused until the edge changes**. If next year a row is pulled or a headland moves, you update that number. You do not start again from the cadastral sheet as if nothing were known.

In practice **the field card has two areas**, named in plain language. The one on the sheet, which serves the land register and the forms. The sprayable one, which serves buying, filling the tank and writing the register. Mixing them up is the mistake. Keeping both, with different names, is the solution offices already apply by hand, and that an archive can stop them redoing.

When a dose suggestion uses the sprayable area, the person confirming can see whether the number matches what they saw in the field. They can raise or lower it, and the line remembers the correction. Next time you do not start from the cadastral 4.2. You start from **the last accepted measure**. That is how a calculation stops being an office exercise and becomes a habit, the kind the British study of farm tools points to as a condition for real use.

It also helps to note why, in a word, when the sprayable area changes: vines pulled, a new buffer, a spray only on the edge. Without that why, next year nobody knows whether the lower number was a choice or a typing error. With that why, the field card is a memory shared by the person who drives and the person who writes the register. They stop arguing about the cadastral sheet and talk about the real edge.

The same card also prevents a swollen purchase. If the product is ordered on the sprayable area, the drum that arrives is the one the season can use, not the one calculated on the whole sheet. Warehouse and register start closer, before the first spray. The person ordering and the person spraying are then looking at the same hectares.

Seminai uses the farm’s fields and production units for this comparison, and leaves confirmation to the person who signs. It does not replace an adviser’s survey the first time the ditch edge has to be measured. It avoids remeasuring it from memory at every pass.

In organic farming the same mistake weighs more, because a product off the list, or copper counted on the wrong area, is not just a line to fix.
